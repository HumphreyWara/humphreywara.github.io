01
EXTRUSION · BINARY Al–Ce

How extrusion transformed a coarse cast alloy into a strong, ductile, thermally stable material.

Grain refinement and fragmentation of Al₁₁Ce₃ were connected to major improvements in strength, ductility, toughness, and high-temperature stability.

EBSD · XRD · SEM · Tensile · Fracture
Read full storyClose story+

The challenge

Cast binary Al–Ce contains a coarse aluminum matrix and a continuous or interconnected Al₁₁Ce₃ intermetallic network. The research question was whether thermomechanical processing could refine this structure without sacrificing the intrinsic thermal stability provided by the Ce-rich phase.

Processing & characterization

The alloy was hot extruded at approximately 300 °C with a large area reduction. SEM and EBSD were used to track fragmentation of the intermetallic phase, grain refinement, and crystallographic texture, while XRD confirmed the principal phases. Tensile and fracture testing connected the evolving microstructure to mechanical behavior.

What the material did

Extrusion refined the aluminum grains from the coarse as-cast scale to roughly the micrometer scale and broke up the Al₁₁Ce₃ network. Room-temperature tensile strength rose to about 250 MPa while ductility increased to roughly 25%. The processed alloy also retained strong mechanical performance after prolonged exposure at 300 °C.

Why it matters

The work showed that Al–Ce alloys can be strengthened through conventional thermomechanical processing while preserving the phase stability needed for elevated-temperature applications. It established the processing–microstructure–property framework that guided the later ternary and multicomponent alloy studies.

02
ALLOY DEVELOPMENT · Al–Ce–Mg

Adding Mg to raise strength while preserving the thermal stability of the Al–Ce system.

Mg solid-solution strengthening was combined with stable Ce-rich intermetallics to create a substantially stronger alloy after extrusion.

Extrusion · Heat treatment · SEM · EBSD · Mechanical testing
Read full storyClose story+

The design idea

The binary Al–Ce system offered excellent thermal stability but limited room-temperature strength. Mg was introduced to add solid-solution strengthening while retaining the thermally stable Al₁₁Ce₃ phase that distinguishes the Al–Ce system.

Processing & characterization

Cast Al–10Ce–4Mg was thermomechanically processed by extrusion and evaluated using SEM, EBSD, XRD, hardness, tensile testing, and fracture measurements. Thermal exposures at 300 °C were used to determine how much of the processed strength survived prolonged heating.

Performance

The extruded ternary alloy reached approximately 390 MPa ultimate tensile strength with about 15% ductility and fracture toughness near 23 kJ/m². After 100 hours at 300 °C, it retained most of its room-temperature strength, demonstrating a useful balance between strengthening and thermal stability.

Why it matters

This study demonstrated a practical alloy-design strategy: use Mg to increase matrix strength and Ce-rich intermetallics to resist microstructural degradation at temperature. The result is an aluminum alloy concept aimed at applications where both low density and sustained elevated-temperature performance are important.

03
ROLLING · SCALABLE PROCESSING

Translating the Al–Ce–Mg concept into a hot-rolling route relevant to sheet processing.

A controlled rolling schedule refined the microstructure and substantially increased strength relative to the as-cast alloy.

Rolling · SEM · XRD · EBSD · Hardness · Tensile
Read full storyClose story+

The objective

Extrusion demonstrated the strengthening potential of the ternary alloy, but rolling is particularly relevant to scalable sheet and plate manufacturing. The goal was to determine whether a repeatable hot-rolling schedule could create comparable microstructural refinement and property improvement.

The route

Al–10Ce–4Mg was rolled through multiple controlled reductions with reheating between passes. Microstructure and phase stability were assessed by SEM, EBSD, and XRD, while hardness and tensile tests quantified the effect of deformation and subsequent thermal exposure.

Results

Approximately 54% total reduction produced a strong improvement over the cast state, with ultimate tensile strength reaching about 269 MPa. After 100 hours at 300 °C, the rolled material retained about 83% of its UTS and approximately 92% of its yield strength.

Why it matters

The work moved the alloy system toward a more industrially scalable processing route and showed that rolling can provide a useful combination of strengthening, microstructural control, and retention of mechanical properties after thermal exposure.

04
RAPID SOLIDIFICATION · Al–Ce–Cu–Zr

Using melt spinning to push refinement and hardness far beyond the conventionally cast condition.

Rapid solidification generated a highly refined multicomponent microstructure and enabled the study of phase evolution during high-temperature aging.

Melt spinning · Annealing · XRD · SEM · Microhardness
Read full storyClose story+

The question

Rapid solidification offers cooling rates far above conventional casting, making it possible to refine phases, extend solubility, and create microstructures that cannot easily be produced by equilibrium processing. The study explored that opportunity in an Al–Ce–Cu–Zr composition.

Processing & characterization

The alloy was produced as thin melt-spun ribbon and then annealed at elevated temperature. XRD and microscopy were used to identify phase evolution, while microhardness tracked the mechanical response of the rapidly solidified and annealed states.

Results

Melt spinning increased hardness to roughly 244 HV compared with about 113 HV in the as-cast condition. The refined microstructure contained α-Al and Ce-rich intermetallic phases, with nanoscale Al₈CeCu₄ and sporadic Al₂CuZr features contributing to the multiscale strengthening response.

Why it matters

The project demonstrated how composition and solidification rate can be used together to engineer high hardness and thermal response. It also broadened the research from conventional bulk thermomechanical processing to nonequilibrium microstructure design.

05
MAGNETIC MATERIALS · NdFeB

Supporting continuous hot-roll processing of nanograin rare-earth permanent magnets.

Hands-on work connected powder preparation, canning, hot rolling, grain-boundary treatment, and magnetic characterization.

Powder processing · Can welding · Hot rolling · Grain-boundary diffusion · Magnetics
Read full storyClose story+

The manufacturing challenge

High-performance NdFeB magnets depend strongly on grain size, texture, interfaces, and processing history. The project focused on continuous hot-roll processing as a route toward dense nanograin magnets with controlled magnetic properties.

My contribution

I supported powder handling and milling, packing, can welding, hot rolling, sample preparation, and magnetic testing. I also participated in grain-boundary diffusion work involving Cu-coated feedstock and process optimization trials.

Outcome

Process optimization produced measurable improvements in coercivity and magnetization, including approximately 3% improvement in key magnetic response during the development work.

Why it matters

This work extended my process-development experience beyond aluminum alloys into rare-earth permanent magnets and reinforced the same core engineering principle: processing controls microstructure, and microstructure controls functional performance.

SEMICONDUCTORS · DEVICE FABRICATION

Building hands-on familiarity with thin-film processing, lithography, and semiconductor devices.

Cleanroom fabrication experience covered the process sequence from wafer preparation and patterning to deposition, etching, metallization, and device testing.

Cleanroom · Photolithography · Thin films · CVD/MOCVD · Ga₂O₃ devices
Read full storyClose story+

The experience

My semiconductor training introduced the full logic of wafer-scale process integration: how individual deposition, patterning, etching, oxidation, diffusion, and metallization steps must work together to create a functional device.

Hands-on work

I gained laboratory experience with silicon wafer fabrication, thin films, CVD-based processing, photolithography and custom masks, PDMS replication for electrochemical sensors, and fabrication of Ga₂O₃ photodetectors.

What transferred to materials R&D

The work strengthened my understanding of surface preparation, process sequencing, thin-film–substrate interactions, defect control, and characterization at small length scales.

Why it matters

Although my primary research focus is physical metallurgy, this experience gives me a broader process-development perspective and makes it easier to work across metallic materials, coatings, thin films, and device-oriented manufacturing environments.
